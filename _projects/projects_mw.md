At the center of the Milky Way (MW, or the<em> Galaxy</em>) sits a supermassive black hole, where stars are still forming (e.g., <a href="https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-3881/ac0534" target="_blank">this</a> is my spectroscopic work on planetary nebulae in this region). Surrounding it, stars make up the young, beautiful spiral arms and the disk. The disk generally comes in two flavors: an older thicker one and a younger thinner one. Farther out, old stars roam the halo. Some formed within the MW, while many were accreted from smaller galaxies over time. The halo also has a complex structure, with inner and outer regions that have distinct characteristics.

All of this tells us that the MW didn’t form all at once, but piece by piece. Since we don’t have a time machine, we rely on the chemistry and dynamics of old stars to figure out how this whole structure came together and evolved.

<strong>Carbon-enhanced metal-poor (CEMP) stars</strong>

Stars with lower metallicity often turn out to have more carbon. That carbon matters — not just for life as we know it, but also because it tells us something about the environments where the star was born. For instance, many CEMP stars with overabundant slow-process neutron capture elements, CEMP-s, are thought to have received mass from their companion stars in binary systems. On the other hand, CEMP-no stars, which don’t show abundant signatures of rapid- or slow-process neutron-capture elements, are believed to be descendants of the first generations in the Universe. Thus, studying these CEMP stars could offer some of the clearest insights when the first stars and galaxies.

Thanks to recent wide-field multi-band photometric surveys, we now have access to hundreds of thousands of potential CEMP stars, because using narrowband filters along with machine learning techniques allows us to estimate the abundances of elements like iron, carbon, and magnesium even without spectra. Hong et al. (2026a, submitted) identified about 105,000 CEMP candidate stars from the J-PLUS and S-PLUS photometric datasets (<a href="https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4357/ad6b94" target="_blank">Huang et al. 2024</a> and Huang et al. 2025, in press). They also attempted to distinguish CEMP-no and CEMP-s stars purely from photometric abundance estimates, examined their frequencies across various Galactic substructures, and inferred aspects of the MW’s evolutionary history.

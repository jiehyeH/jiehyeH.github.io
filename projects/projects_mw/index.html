Milky Way

right where we live

At the quiet center of the Milky Way (MW, or just the Galaxy) sits a supermassive black hole — and yes, stars are still being born nearby. (Here is a spectroscopic study of planetary nebulae in this region that I worked on during my master’s.) Surrounding this region is the disk, home to stars that make up the Galaxy’s beautiful spiral arms — still relatively young. The disk comes in two flavors: an older, thicker one and a younger, thinner one. Zooming farther out, we find ancient stars roaming the halo. Some have always belonged to the MW, while many were pulled in from smaller galaxies over time. You’ll even find a few of these halo stars hiding out in the disk (more on that below). The halo isn’t just a fuzzy cloud — it has structure, with both an inner and outer region. All of this tells us that the MW didn’t form all at once, but piece by piece. Since we don’t have a time machine, we rely on the chemistry and dynamics of old stars to figure out how this whole structure came together and evolved.

Very and extremely metal-poor (VMP/EMP) stars considered as primordial disk candidates from Hong et al. (2024). To define the disk, stars within 3 kpc of the MW midplane (top) or with inclination angles (arctan of Zmax/Rmax, Haywood et al. 2018) below 0.65 (bottom) are considered disk-like. Using AGAMA (Vasiliev 2019) and the SMSS/SAGES photometric data from Huang et al. (2022) and Huang et al. (2023), their orbits show low eccentricities, similar to typical disk stars. This suggests they may trace the earliest disk component in the MW’s history.

Carbon-enhanced metal-poor stars

Stars with lower metallicity often turn out to have more carbon. That carbon matters — not just for life as we know it, but also because it tells us something about the conditions where the star was born. For instance, many carbon-enhanced metal-poor (CEMP) stars in the CEMP-s category are thought to have received material from a companion star that once went through the asymptotic giant branch (AGB) phase before becoming a current white dwarf. That suggests they formed in binary systems. On the other hand, CEMP-no stars, which don’t show abundant signatures of neutron-capture elements, are believed to be direct descendants of the Universe’s first stars — Population III. Studying these stars could offer some of the clearest insights we have into the earliest stellar generations.

Thanks to wide-field photometric surveys, we now have access to millions of potential CEMP stars. Narrow-band filters help us estimate abundances of elements like iron, carbon, and magnesium — even without spectra — especially when combined with modern machine learning techniques. Hong et al. (2025) are examining the distribution of these stars across different Galactic substructures using carbon and iron abundance estimates from Huang et al. (2024) and Huang et al. (2025, in prep), based on J-PLUS and S-PLUS photometry. By adapting the criteria from Yoon et al. (2016), we’re working to distinguish between likely CEMP-no and CEMP-s stars purely from photometric data. The full results are coming soon — stay tuned!
